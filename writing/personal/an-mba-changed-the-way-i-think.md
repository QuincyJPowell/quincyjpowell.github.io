<article class="longform-article">

<p class="article-intro">
Graduating with my Bachelor’s degree in Business was a dream come true. I was the first person in my family to graduate with a college degree, and I did so at an accelerated pace. Unlike most fresh grads, though, I didn't take a break from the academic world to join the workforce.
</p>

<p>
I had the immense good fortune of having been hired on for an incredible internship with AARP Montana as their Social Media Intern for my final semester, the fall term, of my undergraduate degree. Not only was the team amazing to work with, but the role itself changed my life. I felt a calling for the kind of marketing that I was doing for AARP Montana—disseminating useful information to the people most in need of it. I have a genuine passion for the mission of AARP, and I was a good fit for the role.
</p>

<p>
It was mentioned that they would be sad to lose me when I graduated, and I was asked if I was going to be pursuing a graduate degree in hopes that I could stay on longer. In a matter of days, I had pivoted my plans for the next few years by applying and being accepted to my state’s sole MBA program.
</p>

<p>
Though the program I graduated from doesn't have traditional concentrations, I chose to focus my degree path primarily on marketing, which was quite the change from my undergraduate specialty in management. With additional coursework in entrepreneurship and consulting, I chose to focus my degree work on preparing me for a career in marketing because my internship had inspired me.
</p>

<p>
I considered myself well-prepared for the program, at least at the time. I had flown through my undergraduate degree with few issues and have always had a love for school and learning. My Master’s program stretched my mind in ways I had not expected, however, and exposed me to opportunities that have forever shifted the way I think.
</p>

<h2>The Shift Away From Textbooks</h2>

<p>
I spent my undergraduate degree carefully budgeting for and thoroughly reading a wide variety of textbooks. To say that I was surprised to see not a single textbook required or even recommended for my first semester of my graduate program would be an understatement.
</p>

<p>
A few of my classes did use textbooks, but the readings I relied upon during my coursework were overwhelmingly real-world case studies and timely business news. Current peer-reviewed articles from recent months were featured in that prime syllabus readings spot. My professors were not only providing interesting material, but many of them were also sourcing new material from our research.
</p>

<p>
I don't want to make the mistake of implying that textbooks aren’t useful. They’re a fantastic foundation! And honestly, I’ve always been the kid that genuinely gets a kick out of reading textbooks. But I very much wish that my undergraduate program had relied a bit less on textbooks and a bit more on relevant and timely research, for more than financial reasons!
</p>

<p>
My classes were rapidly adapting to real-world changes. I remember occasions where my professors would scrap a lecture last-minute just to discuss a more groundbreaking development, especially in the case of ChatGPT and generative AI.
</p>

<p>
This kind of flexibility just wouldn't have worked in a course following alongside a textbook. It would have been too challenging to pivot in response to change.
</p>

<h2>Collaboration and Problem-Solving</h2>

<p>
Nearly every college course today has something in the syllabus about developing critical thinking skills, as they should, but I personally felt a big difference in this between my programs, and not in how my professors approached it.
</p>

<p>
The difference was, I believe, the attitude and drive of the students involved. With college courses for an undergraduate degree, especially the lower-level courses, you’re still in class with students whose mettle is actively being tested, students who may still wash out. I think especially with a business degree, which is seen as a malleable catch-all degree useful for those who don’t quite have things figured out—I certainly fell into this mindset at one time—this can be a dangerous trap for students.
</p>

<p>
Being passionate about your classes really makes a difference in how well you learn. When you aren’t interested in the material because it’s a class you have to take for a degree that doesn’t excite you, it suddenly becomes much harder to focus on readings, lectures, or studying. Declaring a business degree just because you can probably use it for just about anything is a pretty good recipe for ending up bored and struggling in at least a few classes.
</p>

<p>
In my undergraduate program, I also received an Interdisciplinary Honors Minor and a History Minor. In retrospect, the collaborative atmosphere I appreciated so much in my history classes—which, ironically, helped me power through my boring business classes at the time—was much closer to the type of interactive environment I experienced in my graduate classes.
</p>

<p>
I was surrounded by students who had already graduated with their degrees, many of whom had been in the workforce for many years. Nearly all of us were working a job of some sort, and while we all had different goals and approaches, we all had a similar kind of drive and ambition which made it easier to work together. And don’t even get me started on the group projects! There really was no bad apple as far as group work went, which endlessly impressed me!
</p>

<p>
Funnily enough, you do get out what you put into your studies. In writing this, I do find myself wishing that I had put more effort into my studies. There isn’t even a particular moment that I can look at and say, “ah, this is when I should have worked harder,” but I think I am left wondering what would have happened had I taken my time, taken more classes, explored more topics more thoroughly, and been able to devote myself fully to academic pursuit.
</p>

<p>
Though I miss the structure of an educational program, we are lifelong learners. That’s certainly another way my thinking changed due to my graduate program: missing school!
</p>

<h2>The Anatomy of a Problem</h2>

<p>
Truly, even the way I think of problems has completely changed. It was a gradual development from problems have solutions, to problems are nuanced and multifaceted and sometimes they can't be solved, to problems are nuanced and multifaceted, but they're also opportunities for improvement, for growth, for change, and sometimes the cost of solving a problem in any particular way can outweigh the benefits.
</p>

<p>
There are so many ways to think about problems and solutions and opportunities, and they're all an interconnected web that's all tangled together. Not just when it comes to the business world! The way we think about things has a profound impact on how we talk, how we strategize, how we take action, in every aspect of our lives across the board.
</p>

<p>
I think the most profound idea I have been trying to apply to my own life is this: a problem is just an opportunity making itself known. Once I figure out that I have a problem, my job is to then identify the highest-benefit, lowest-cost opportunity that addresses that problem. It's touch-and-go, don't get me wrong, but in changing the way I think, I can change the way I act and therefore the way I exist.
</p>

<p>
Usually, when people talk about an MBA, they talk about it strictly in a cost-benefit analysis type of way. As in “the cost of the degree and projected lost time” versus “projected future earnings and prestige” and that’s certainly part of the discussion I had with my family when I chose to go for my Master’s. I haven't really seen a tangible benefit from going out and getting my graduate degree yet.
</p>

<p>
Even so, I don't regret it. Every experience changes you, and putting the time and effort into such rigorous study has a profound impact. It’s all of the intangible benefits you gain, all of the things you experience and learn, that really made my degree experience a life-changing one.
</p>
